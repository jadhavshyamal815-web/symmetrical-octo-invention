# Preclinical Evidence Map

## 1. Introduction

This document provides a structured map of the major evidence categories
used in biosimilarity assessment and related preclinical research.

The purpose of this evidence map is to explain:

- Why each evidence category is generated.
- What information is used as input.
- What is measured.
- What type of evidence is expected.
- How evidence quality can be controlled.
- How evidence should be interpreted.
- What limitations exist.
- What the evidence does NOT prove.

The evidence map is intended for scientific learning, evidence
organization, and quality-control planning.

It is not a regulatory approval system and does not determine whether a
candidate product is biosimilar.

# 2. Overall Biosimilarity Evidence Framework

A simplified evidence workflow can be represented as:

```text
Reference Product
        +
Candidate Product
        ↓
Analytical Evidence
        ↓
Structural Evidence
        ↓
Functional Evidence
        ↓
PK / PD Evidence
        ↓
Immunogenicity
        ↓
Preclinical Evidence
        ↓
Clinical Evidence
        ↓
Totality of Evidence
        ↓
Human Scientific Review
Evidence Category 1 — Analytical Evidence
Purpose
Analytical evidence is used to compare important physicochemical and
molecular characteristics of the candidate product with those of the
reference product.
The objective is to identify whether the candidate is highly similar to
the reference product with respect to relevant quality attributes.
Input
Typical inputs include:
- Reference product samples
- Candidate product samples
- Product specifications
- Relevant analytical methods
- Critical quality attributes
- Reference information
Measurement
Depending on the biological product, measurements may include:
- Chromatographic analysis
- Mass spectrometry
- Electrophoresis
- Spectroscopic analysis
- Protein characterization
- Purity-related measurements
- Aggregation-related measurements
- Charge-variant analysis
- Glycosylation analysis
Expected Evidence
Expected evidence is a scientifically justified pattern of high
similarity between relevant analytical characteristics of the candidate
and reference product.
The objective is not necessarily that every measurement is numerically
identical.
Biological products can show variability, and differences must be
evaluated for their scientific relevance.
Quality-Control Considerations
Important QC considerations include:
- Appropriate analytical method
- Method suitability
- Method validation or qualification status
- Sample identification
- Reference and candidate traceability
- Instrument status
- Data completeness
- Appropriate controls
- Repeatability and reproducibility
- Documentation of deviations
- Raw-data availability
Interpretation
Analytical evidence can provide strong information about molecular and
physicochemical similarity.
It forms an important foundation for the overall biosimilarity evidence
package.
Limitations
Analytical measurements do not capture every biological or clinical
property.
Some biological characteristics may require functional, PK, PD,
immunogenicity, preclinical, or clinical evidence.
What It Does NOT Prove
Analytical evidence alone does NOT prove:
- Complete clinical equivalence
- Clinical efficacy
- Complete clinical safety
- Absence of all immunogenicity differences
- Identical patient outcomes
4. Evidence Category 2 — Structural Evidence
Purpose
Structural evidence evaluates whether important molecular structural
characteristics of the candidate product are similar to those of the
reference product.
Input
Inputs may include:
- Candidate product
- Reference product
- Product samples
- Structural characteristics
- Critical quality attributes
- Appropriate analytical methods
Measurement
Measurements may examine:
- Primary structure
- Higher-order structure
- Molecular size
- Protein folding
- Glycosylation
- Charge characteristics
- Aggregation
- Purity
- Other product-specific structural attributes
Expected Evidence
Expected evidence consists of scientifically acceptable similarity in
relevant structural characteristics.
The interpretation should consider the natural variability and
complexity of biological products.
Quality-Control Considerations
QC should consider:
- Sample identity
- Sample integrity
- Appropriate analytical method
- Method validation or qualification
- Instrument performance
- Calibration where applicable
- Data completeness
- Traceability
- Appropriate controls
- Documentation of deviations
- Reproducibility
Interpretation
Structural evidence helps determine whether the candidate and reference
products have comparable molecular characteristics.
Structural information can support the interpretation of functional and
other evidence.
Limitations
Structural characterization cannot represent every possible biological
activity or clinical response.
Some differences may require functional or other evidence to determine
their relevance.
What It Does NOT Prove
Structural evidence alone does NOT prove:
- Clinical efficacy
- Complete clinical safety
- Identical pharmacokinetics
- Identical pharmacodynamics
- Absence of immunogenicity
- Complete clinical equivalence
5. Evidence Category 3 — Functional Evidence
Purpose
Functional evidence evaluates whether the candidate product performs
relevant biological functions in a manner comparable to the reference
product.
Input
Inputs may include:
- Candidate product
- Reference product
- Appropriate biological systems
- Product-specific functional assays
- Relevant controls
Measurement
Depending on the product, functional measurements may include:
- Receptor binding
- Enzyme activity
- Biological activity
- Cell-based activity
- Binding affinity
- Potency
- Mechanism-related activity
Expected Evidence
The expected evidence is comparable biological activity between the
candidate and reference product using scientifically appropriate assays.
Quality-Control Considerations
Important considerations include:
- Suitability of the assay
- Method validation or qualification
- Appropriate controls
- Sample traceability
- Assay performance
- Measurement variability
- Raw-data availability
- Reproducibility
- Documentation of deviations
- Appropriate statistical analysis
Interpretation
Functional evidence helps determine whether molecular and structural
similarity is associated with comparable biological activity.
It can provide complementary information to analytical and structural
evidence.
Limitations
A particular functional assay measures a specific biological property.
One assay cannot necessarily represent every biological mechanism or
clinical effect.
What It Does NOT Prove
Functional evidence alone does NOT prove:
- Complete clinical efficacy
- Complete clinical safety
- Identical patient outcomes
- Absence of all immunogenicity
- Regulatory approval
6. Evidence Category 4 — Pharmacokinetic (PK) Evidence
Purpose
Pharmacokinetic evidence evaluates how the body handles the candidate
and reference products.
It may be used to compare systemic exposure when appropriate.
Input
Typical inputs may include:
- Candidate product
- Reference product
- Product exposure information
- Concentration measurements
- Appropriate study data
Measurement
PK measurements can include:
- Drug concentration over time
- Area under the curve (AUC)
- Maximum concentration (Cmax)
- Time to maximum concentration (Tmax)
- Half-life
- Other relevant PK parameters
Expected Evidence
Expected evidence is an appropriate comparison of relevant PK
characteristics between the candidate and reference product.
The interpretation depends on the study design and applicable scientific
and regulatory criteria.
Quality-Control Considerations
QC considerations include:
- Sample identification
- Validated or appropriately qualified analytical method
- Accurate concentration measurements
- Data completeness
- Appropriate study documentation
- Appropriate statistical analysis
- Handling of missing data
- Protocol deviations
- Traceability
Interpretation
PK evidence can help determine whether systemic exposure to the
candidate is comparable with that of the reference product.
Limitations
PK evidence primarily describes exposure and disposition.
It does not represent every aspect of biological activity or clinical
response.
What It Does NOT Prove
PK evidence alone does NOT prove:
- Complete clinical efficacy
- Complete clinical safety
- Identical pharmacodynamics
- Absence of immunogenicity
- Complete clinical equivalence
7. Evidence Category 5 — Pharmacodynamic (PD) Evidence
Purpose
Pharmacodynamic evidence evaluates biological or physiological responses
associated with the product.
It can help compare the biological response of the candidate and
reference product where a suitable PD measure exists.
Input
Inputs may include:
- Candidate product
- Reference product
- Relevant biological marker
- Physiological response
- Product-specific PD information
Measurement
Measurements may include:
- Biomarker response
- Biological activity
- Physiological response
- Mechanism-related marker
- Other scientifically justified PD endpoints
Expected Evidence
Expected evidence is a comparable PD response between the candidate and
reference product where the selected PD measure is relevant and
sufficiently sensitive.
Quality-Control Considerations
Important QC considerations include:
- Appropriate PD endpoint
- Measurement-method suitability
- Assay validation or qualification
- Sample identification
- Appropriate controls
- Data completeness
- Statistical analysis
- Variability assessment
- Protocol compliance
- Traceability
Interpretation
PD evidence can provide information about biological response and may
complement PK and other evidence.
Limitations
A PD endpoint may represent only one biological pathway or response.
The relationship between a PD marker and clinical outcomes must be
considered carefully.
What It Does NOT Prove
PD evidence alone does NOT prove:
- Complete clinical efficacy
- Complete clinical safety
- Identical patient outcomes
- Absence of all immune responses
- Regulatory approval
8. Evidence Category 6 — Immunogenicity
Purpose
Immunogenicity evidence evaluates the immune response associated with a
biological product.
This is important because biological products can induce immune
responses that may affect safety, exposure, or biological activity.
Input
Inputs may include:
- Candidate product
- Reference product
- Appropriate biological samples
- Immunogenicity assays
- Anti-drug antibody information
- Neutralizing antibody information where relevant
Measurement
Measurements may include:
- Anti-drug antibodies
- Neutralizing antibodies
- Other relevant immune responses
- Incidence or characteristics of immune responses
Expected Evidence
Expected evidence is a scientifically appropriate comparison of
immunogenicity-related characteristics between candidate and reference
products.
Quality-Control Considerations
Important QC considerations include:
- Appropriate assay
- Assay validation
- Sample traceability
- Appropriate controls
- Assay sensitivity
- Data completeness
- Timing and context of measurements
- Appropriate statistical reporting
- Handling of missing information
- Reproducibility
Interpretation
Immunogenicity evidence can help evaluate whether the candidate and
reference products have comparable immune-response characteristics.
Limitations
Immune responses can vary between individuals and populations.
Interpretation depends on the assay, timing, product, study population,
and other scientific factors.
What It Does NOT Prove
Immunogenicity evidence alone does NOT prove:
- Complete clinical safety
- Complete clinical efficacy
- Absence of all adverse reactions
- Long-term safety in every population
- Regulatory approval
9. Evidence Category 7 — Preclinical Evidence
Purpose
Preclinical evidence provides non-clinical information about biological
activity, pharmacology, safety, or other relevant characteristics before
or outside clinical research.
Depending on the scientific question, preclinical evidence can include
in-vitro and in-vivo studies.
Input
Inputs may include:
- Candidate product
- Reference product
- Study protocol
- Study reports
- Experimental observations
- Analytical measurements
- Biological measurements
- Safety-related observations
Measurement
Measurements may include:
- Biological activity
- Pharmacological responses
- Biomarkers
- Toxicological observations
- Other scientifically justified endpoints
Expected Evidence
Expected evidence should be:
- Scientifically relevant
- Appropriately documented
- Traceable
- Reproducible where applicable
- Supported by appropriate controls
- Consistent with the study objective
Quality-Control Considerations
QC considerations include:
- Study identification
- Protocol/reference documentation
- Sample identification
- Control identification
- Experimental conditions
- Measurement methods
- Method validation or qualification status
- Raw-data availability
- Missing data
- Protocol deviations
- Statistical reporting
- Reproducibility
- Data integrity
- Ethical oversight
- Reviewer assessment
Interpretation
Preclinical evidence can provide useful scientific information about
the biological or safety-related characteristics of a product.
However, the relevance of each finding must be considered within the
overall evidence framework.
Limitations
Preclinical findings may not fully predict human clinical outcomes.
The relevance of an animal or laboratory model depends on the scientific
question and model characteristics.
What It Does NOT Prove
Preclinical evidence alone does NOT prove:
- Complete human safety
- Complete clinical efficacy
- Biosimilarity
- Identical patient outcomes
- Regulatory approval
10. Evidence Category 8 — Clinical Evidence
Purpose
Clinical evidence provides information about the product in human
subjects and can address clinical pharmacology, safety, efficacy, and
immunogenicity as applicable.
Input
Inputs may include:
- Clinical study data
- Candidate product
- Reference product
- Human participant information
- Clinical endpoints
- PK/PD information
- Safety data
- Immunogenicity data
Measurement
Measurements may include:
- Clinical endpoints
- Safety outcomes
- PK parameters
- PD markers
- Immunogenicity
- Adverse events
- Other clinically relevant outcomes
Expected Evidence
Expected evidence should be generated using appropriate scientific and
clinical study designs and should be appropriately documented and
analyzed.
Quality-Control Considerations
Important considerations include:
- Protocol compliance
- Participant/sample identification
- Data integrity
- Appropriate endpoint definitions
- Statistical analysis
- Missing data
- Protocol deviations
- Safety reporting
- Appropriate review
- Traceability
Interpretation
Clinical evidence provides information about human responses to the
candidate product.
It is interpreted together with analytical, functional,
pharmacological, immunogenicity, and other relevant evidence.
Limitations
Clinical studies have defined populations, durations, endpoints, and
study conditions.
They cannot automatically establish every possible long-term or
population-specific outcome.
What It Does NOT Prove
Clinical evidence from an individual study does NOT automatically prove:
- Every possible long-term safety outcome
- Complete absence of rare adverse events
- Suitability for every population
- Regulatory approval
11. Summary of the Eight Evidence Categories
Evidence Category	Main Purpose	Main Limitation
Analytical	Compare molecular and physicochemical characteristics	Does not independently establish clinical outcomes
Structural	Compare molecular structure and attributes	Does not independently establish clinical safety or efficacy
Functional	Compare biological activity	Does not represent every clinical outcome
PK	Compare systemic exposure	Does not independently establish complete clinical efficacy
PD	Compare biological response	Depends on relevance and sensitivity of the PD marker
Immunogenicity	Compare immune responses	Does not independently establish complete safety
Preclinical	Provide non-clinical scientific information	May not fully predict human outcomes
Clinical	Evaluate human responses	Individual studies have defined scope and limitations


12. Preclinical and Animal-Study Evidence
12.1 Study Objective
Every scientific study should have a clearly defined objective.
The objective describes what scientific question the study is intended
to address.
A well-defined objective helps determine:
- Appropriate study design
- Relevant evidence
- Suitable measurements
- Appropriate endpoints
- Appropriate interpretation
The objective should be documented before the evidence is evaluated.
12.2 Study Design
Study design describes the overall scientific structure used to answer
the research question.
At a conceptual level, study design may include:
- Study objective
- Study population or model
- Experimental groups
- Control groups
- Relevant measurements
- Endpoints
- Observation period
- Data-analysis plan
- Reporting requirements
The design should be appropriate for the scientific question.
This project does not provide operational instructions for conducting
animal experiments.
12.3 Experimental Groups
Experimental groups are groups exposed to the conditions relevant to
the scientific question.
The groups should be clearly identified in the study documentation.
Important information includes:
- Group identification
- Study condition
- Sample identification
- Number of observations
- Relevant study period
The exact experimental setup depends on the scientific objective.
12.4 Control Groups
Control groups provide a basis for interpreting observations.
A control may be used to determine whether an observed difference is
associated with the investigated factor rather than another source of
variation.
Controls should be scientifically justified and clearly documented.
The existence of a control does not automatically establish study
quality.
12.5 Sample Identification
Samples should have clear identifiers that allow them to be traced
through the study and associated records.
Important documentation may include:
- Sample ID
- Product identification
- Study ID
- Group identification
- Collection or observation information
- Storage or handling records where applicable
Good identification supports data integrity and traceability.
12.6 Dosing Information
Dosing information is an important part of study documentation when a
study involves administration of a product.
At a conceptual documentation level, relevant records may include:
- Product identity
- Dose information
- Administration information
- Group assignment
- Relevant timing information
This project does not provide operational instructions for animal
dosing or experimental administration.
12.7 Observation Period
The observation period defines the period during which relevant study
observations are recorded.
It should be appropriate to the scientific objective and documented
clearly.
The observation period can influence the interpretation of findings,
especially when effects may change over time.
12.8 Endpoints
Endpoints are predefined observations or measurements used to address
the study objective.
Examples of conceptual endpoint types include:
- Biological response
- Pharmacological response
- Biomarker measurement
- Safety-related observation
- Histological or other scientific observations where appropriate
Endpoints should be clearly defined and consistently documented.
12.9 Measurements
Measurements are the observations or data collected during a study.
Measurement quality can be affected by:
- Method suitability
- Instrument performance
- Sample quality
- Measurement variability
- Operator effects
- Data recording
- Data processing
The measurement method should be documented sufficiently to support
review and traceability.
12.10 Adverse Observations
Adverse observations are unwanted or harmful observations identified
during a study.
They should be:
- Recorded accurately
- Associated with the relevant subject/sample where appropriate
- Documented consistently
- Reviewed in context
- Reported according to applicable study requirements
An adverse observation should not automatically be attributed to the
candidate product without appropriate scientific evaluation.
12.11 Statistical Considerations
Statistical considerations help determine how study data should be
summarized and interpreted.
Conceptual considerations include:
- Study objective
- Sample size justification
- Variability
- Appropriate statistical methods
- Missing data
- Outliers
- Confidence intervals where appropriate
- Effect estimates
- Predefined analysis plans
Statistical significance alone does not automatically establish
scientific or clinical importance.
12.12 Protocol Deviations
A protocol deviation is a departure from the planned study procedures
or requirements.
Deviations should be:
- Documented
- Assessed
- Reviewed for potential impact
- Reported appropriately
A deviation does not automatically invalidate a study.
Its significance depends on what happened and how it may affect the
evidence.
12.13 Data Integrity
Data integrity is essential for reliable scientific evidence.
Important principles include:
- Accurate records
- Complete records
- Traceability
- Consistency
- Appropriate documentation
- Secure data handling
- Controlled changes
- Reviewability
Evidence without reliable data integrity may have reduced confidence.
12.14 Reproducibility
Reproducibility concerns whether scientifically appropriate repetition
can produce consistent findings.
Factors affecting reproducibility may include:
- Biological variability
- Study design
- Measurement method
- Instrument variation
- Sample variation
- Environmental conditions
- Data analysis
Reproducibility should be considered when assessing the quality and
strength of evidence.
12.15 Ethical Oversight
Animal-related research requires appropriate ethical consideration and
oversight.
Important principles include:
- Scientific justification
- Appropriate ethical review
- Humane treatment
- Minimization of unnecessary harm
- Appropriate documentation
- Compliance with applicable requirements
This project does not design or conduct an animal study.
12.16 Study Reporting
A study report should allow an appropriate reviewer to understand:
- Why the study was conducted
- How it was designed
- What was measured
- What was observed
- What deviations occurred
- How the data were analyzed
- What limitations exist
- How the findings were interpreted
Clear reporting improves traceability and scientific review.
13. Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)
Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) is a quality system concerned with the
organization, conduct, monitoring, recording, reporting, and archiving
of certain non-clinical laboratory studies.
GLP supports confidence in the reliability and integrity of study
records.
Conceptually, GLP is concerned with areas such as:
- Organization and responsibilities
- Study protocols
- Standard operating procedures
- Facilities
- Equipment
- Test and reference materials
- Raw data
- Quality assurance
- Reporting
- Archiving
Why GLP Matters
GLP helps ensure that non-clinical study records are generated and
maintained using controlled and documented processes.
GLP does not mean that every scientific conclusion is automatically
correct.
It primarily supports the quality, traceability, and integrity of the
study process and records.
14. ARRIVE Guidelines
ARRIVE stands for:
Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments
The ARRIVE guidelines provide recommendations for reporting animal
research clearly and completely.
The guidelines help researchers communicate important information about
animal studies so that readers can understand and evaluate the research.
Important reporting concepts include:
- Study design
- Sample characteristics
- Experimental procedures
- Outcome measures
- Statistical analysis
- Results
- Limitations
- Ethical considerations
Why ARRIVE Matters
Good reporting improves:
- Transparency
- Reproducibility
- Scientific interpretation
- Evaluation of study quality
ARRIVE is primarily a reporting framework rather than a replacement for
ethical review or laboratory quality systems.
15. The 3Rs of Animal Research
The 3Rs are:
Replacement
Reduction
Refinement
Replacement
Replacement means using alternatives to animals where scientifically
possible and appropriate.
Examples can include:
- In-vitro systems
- Computational approaches
- Other scientifically justified alternatives
Reduction
Reduction means obtaining scientifically useful information while
avoiding unnecessary use of animals.
The principle focuses on appropriate study planning and avoiding
unnecessary duplication.
Refinement
Refinement means improving procedures and care to reduce pain,
suffering, distress, or other adverse effects.
Why the 3Rs Matter
The 3Rs provide an ethical framework for responsible animal research.
They are based on the principle that animal use should be scientifically
justified and conducted with appropriate consideration for animal
welfare.
16. Preclinical Evidence Quality Assessment
Preclinical evidence should be evaluated using multiple quality
dimensions rather than a single label.
Important dimensions include:
Quality Dimension	Question
Study objective	Is the scientific question clearly defined?
Study design	Is the design appropriate for the objective?
Groups	Are groups clearly identified?
Controls	Are appropriate controls documented?
Samples	Are samples traceable?
Measurements	Are measurement methods documented?
Validation	Is method validation/qualification status known?
Raw data	Is supporting raw data available?
Missing data	Are missing observations documented?
Deviations	Are deviations recorded and assessed?
Statistics	Is statistical reporting adequate?
Reproducibility	Is consistency supported?
Data integrity	Are records reliable and traceable?
Ethics	Is ethical oversight documented where relevant?
Reporting	Is the study sufficiently reported?


17. Evidence Quality Statuses
The project uses the following statuses for organizing evidence:
VERIFIED
The available record has been checked against the defined project
quality requirements and no major documentation problem has been
identified.
PARTIAL
Some required information is available, but the record is incomplete.
NEEDS REVIEW
The record requires additional human review before it can be considered
adequately documented.
CONFLICTING
Different pieces of information appear inconsistent or contradictory.
INSUFFICIENT
The available information is not sufficient for the intended
evidence-organization purpose.
Important Rule
These statuses are organizational labels.
They must NOT be converted directly into scientific, clinical, or
regulatory conclusions.
For example:
VERIFIED
does not mean:
Biosimilar approved
Similarly:
INSUFFICIENT
does not mean:
Product is unsafe
18. Evidence Limitations
Every evidence record should include limitations.
Examples include:
- Small or limited dataset
- Missing information
- Incomplete study documentation
- Method limitations
- Measurement variability
- Unclear control information
- Missing raw data
- Protocol deviations
- Limited reproducibility information
- Limited applicability
- Conflicting observations
Documenting limitations prevents overinterpretation.
19. What the Evidence Map Does NOT Do
This evidence map does NOT:
- Approve a biosimilar.
- Determine clinical efficacy.
- Determine clinical safety.
- Replace regulatory assessment.
- Replace qualified scientific reviewers.
- Replace laboratory quality systems.
- Replace statistical analysis.
- Replace clinical evaluation.
- Design animal experiments.
- Provide operational animal-study instructions.
- Establish that a candidate product is biosimilar.
The purpose is to create a structured framework for organizing and
understanding evidence.

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

23. Key Learning Points
The main lessons from this evidence map are:
1. Biosimilarity is based on a structured body of evidence.
2. Analytical evidence is a major foundation of biosimilar evaluation.
3. Structural and functional evidence answer different questions.
4. PK and PD provide different types of pharmacological information.
5. Immunogenicity is an important consideration for biological products.
6. Preclinical evidence provides non-clinical information.
7. Animal-study evidence requires appropriate scientific and ethical
   consideration.
8. GLP supports study quality and data integrity.
9. ARRIVE supports transparent reporting of animal research.
10. The 3Rs provide an ethical framework for animal research.
11. Controls and endpoints must be appropriate to the scientific
    question.
12. Protocol deviations should be documented and assessed.
13. Missing data and limitations should not be hidden.
14. Reproducibility is important for evidence confidence.
15. Evidence quality should be assessed using multiple dimensions.
16. A quality status is not the same as a scientific conclusion.
17. No single evidence category independently proves biosimilarity.
18. Human scientific review remains essential.
24. Final Statement
The Preclinical Evidence Map provides a structured framework for
understanding how biosimilarity and preclinical evidence can be
generated, documented, quality-controlled, compared, and interpreted.
The central principle is:
Evidence
   ↓
Quality Control
   ↓
Documentation
   ↓
Comparison
   ↓
Limitations
   ↓
Human Review
The framework is intended to support responsible scientific reasoning
and evidence organization.
This is an evidence-organisation framework, not a regulatory approval
system.
